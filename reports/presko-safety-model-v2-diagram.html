How Safety Model v2.1 decides if children can be in school

Rule v1 added up points. v2.1 estimates the actual heat inside each classroom, hour by hour, and checks it against limits set for children. It speaks DepEd's 4H language, refuses to send children out into extreme heat, learns from the indoor readings schools already take, and writes down the reason for every closure as EO 130 asks. 1

Forecast → DepEd 4H tier

The PAGASA forecast heat index for the day sets the tier from DepEd Memorandum 064, s. 2026. The tier is what principals and the division already talk in. The model also keeps the hour-by-hour curve, because heat inside a classroom follows the hours, not the daily peak.

Hinay27–32
Hinga33–41
Hinto≥ 42
HayoRegular face-to-face classes, with precautions
HinayShortened day only: 06:00–10:00 and 14:00–18:00, hybrid or home-based
HingaNo face-to-face (also when the school has no water); light home-based work. The model can give grounds to ask the SDO for classes in rooms that stay cool.
HintoAll academic activity suspended, including home-based

2

Day veto: no school at all

If the forecast heat index reaches 52 °C (PAGASA "extreme danger: heat stroke imminent"), no classroom estimate can make the day safe. This is not hypothetical: the heat index has reached 53 °C (2024) and 60 °C (2023).

Worked example
43 °C < 52 °C → no day veto, continue
3

Commute veto: per session

Children walk to and from school in the sun, often at midday. The model takes the heat index at arrival and dismissal hours and adds up to 4 °C for sun (full sun can add 8 °C; it assumes half the walk is in sun). If that reaches 50 °C, the session is cancelled whatever the building.

Worst commute: 46.9 °C at 11:00 (midday changeover) 46.9 < 50 → no commute veto

In real Iloilo weather for 2023–2025 this veto never fired; the worst day reached about 49 °C. It is a safety net, not the usual reason for closing.

4

Estimate the heat inside each classroom

For every building and every class hour, the model starts from the outdoor temperature and adds what the building and the class do to it.

indoor °C = outdoor °C + building effects (roof, floor, walls, airflow, shade) + learners' body heat (0.025 °C × learners; 40 ≈ +1.0) + learned correction (step 5) + 1 °C margin (Hinto day, no readings yet)

5

Learn from the school's own readings

Memo 064 already asks schools to read indoor temperature at 08:00, 12:00 and 14:00. Each reading is compared with the model's estimate for that hour. The average gap becomes the building's correction. It's held near zero while readings are few, so one odd reading can't swing a decision. Once a building has readings, the 1 °C "unknown" margin is dropped.

correction = Σ (measured − estimated) / (readings + 3)

This is the part of the model that trains: every school day, each building's estimate gets closer to how that building actually behaves. Wrong or outdated building records (a new ceiling, new fans, a blocked window) fix themselves.

Worked example · simulated readings, real weather

Records say Building A has a bare metal roof, but suppose a ceiling was fitted after the survey, so the room really runs about 3 °C cooler.

6

Check each room against limits for children

The estimate is turned into WBGT (wet-bulb globe temperature), the heat-stress measure health guidance uses because it accounts for humidity: sweat can't cool a child when the air is wet. Classes are sedentary, but children are less able to shed heat and do not always drink.

below 28: normal
28–31
≥ 31
28–31: class runs with water and rest breaks. ≥ 31 in any hour: the room is out for that session (Japan's Ministry of the Environment stops children's activity at 31; ISO 7243 puts light work at about 30).

7

Decide, and write down why

Full day if the full-day session is safe; otherwise morning, then afternoon; otherwise closed. The planner then fits sections into the usable rooms as in Rule v1. Every closure carries the documented, site-specific reason EO 130 asks for: which building, which hour, what estimate. The principal confirms or overrides with a reason.

Saying it in 30 seconds

  1. We read PAGASA's forecast hour by hour and put the day in DepEd's 4H tier.
  2. If it's extreme danger, or too hot for children to walk home, there's no school. That's not negotiable.
  3. Otherwise, for every classroom building we estimate how hot it will be inside, hour by hour, from its roof, walls, airflow and how many children are in it.
  4. A room is used only while it stays under the heat-stress limit for children. The day runs in whichever session has safe rooms.
  5. The 08:00, 12:00 and 14:00 readings schools already take teach the model how each building really behaves, so it gets more accurate every day. Every closure comes with the reason, building by building.

What to be ready for in Q&A

  • Why not train on past closures? Because they don't follow the heat: in 2024, Iloilo's suspended days were no hotter than its open days. What we can learn from is each building's readings.
  • Most building effects are assumptions. Only the metal-roof and ceiling effects are measured, and in Ghana. Readings replace the assumptions building by building.
  • The limit is the policy choice. Moving the unsafe line from WBGT 31 to 30 roughly doubles closures in the 2024 replay; 32 halves them. DepEd and health officials should set it.
  • Correcting to a forecast inherits its error. On 3 Nov, matching PAGASA's 43 °C needs an outdoor peak of 36.3 °C, high for November.
  • The 3 Nov comparison uses illustrative buildings. It shows what the model would do, not what was right.
  • Health is not modelled directly. Hydration, illness, uniforms and acclimatisation all shift a child's real risk.
